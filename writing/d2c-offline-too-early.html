Why D2C brands go offline too early

Rent rising faster than revenue, a cash cycle that stretches from fifteen days to a hundred and twenty, and one question that almost nobody answers before signing the first lease.

Preetam Priyabrat October 2026 14 min read All figures sourced below

In the first half of 2026, D2C brands took 28% of all retail leasing in India. Two years earlier that figure was 8%.1,2 Something is happening, and the people doing it are mostly describing it the same way: we had to go omnichannel.

I have been on the small end of this. I run a seasoning brand out of Indore and a phone accessories brand out of Gurugram, and the pull towards a physical presence is real and almost emotional. You stand at a pop-up, you watch somebody pick up your jar, turn it over, read the back, and buy it without you paying anyone for the click. It feels like the thing you were supposed to be doing all along. Our first Gripply pop-up sold out in a day. I remember thinking, quite seriously, that we should find a store.

Then I went and read some filings.

What a store actually costs

The disclosed numbers are more varied than you would expect, and the variation is itself the lesson.

Capex per store from company filings. Format matters enormously, which is why a single "cost per store" figure is close to meaningless.

Notice what is consistent across all of them. The lease commitment is roughly twice the capex. The number brands talk about publicly is the small one.

And lease terms have moved against tenants in a way that is not widely discussed. Average lease tenure in Indian retail fell from 75 months in 2021 to 70 months in the first half of 2026, while the lock-in period rose from 22.2 months to 28.6. As a share of the lease, lock-in went from 29.6% to 40.9%.7 Landlords shortened the lease and lengthened the handcuffs. If you are opening a store to test a format, the option value of being wrong has roughly halved in five years.

The question nobody asks first

Lenskart published the single most useful disclosure in Indian D2C on their Q2 FY26 call, and I think most people read past it.

"Our store level EBITDA in India is about 33%, including opening large number of new stores, versus our EBITDA pre-Ind AS margin of 13%." Peyush Bansal, Lenskart Q2 FY26 earnings call, December 2025.8

Twenty points. Twenty percentage points of a store's contribution get consumed by corporate overhead, central marketing, supply chain and the rest of the apparatus that a store estate requires. That is the empirical answer to what omnichannel actually costs, and it comes from the best operator in the category.

Most brands evaluating their first store model the store. They build a store P&L, it looks fine, they sign. What they have not modelled is the organisation the store requires: the regional manager you will hire at store seven, the warehouse you will need at store twelve, the merchandising function that does not exist yet, the finance person who reconciles twenty landlords.

If a founder cannot tell me both numbers, store-level contribution and the company margin they expect it to produce, I do not think they are ready. Lenskart can tell you. Almost nobody else can.

Payback, and the three different things people mean by it

This is where the conversation gets genuinely slippery, because three companies will quote you three numbers that are not measuring the same thing.

  • BlueStone's RHP says 75% of stores break even within three months.6 That is operational breakeven, the store P&L turning positive.
  • Nykaa's Anchit Nayar says stores "tend to break even usually within the first year or less than that even."9 Also operational.
  • Lenskart's RHP says 81% of stores opened in FY23 and FY24 achieved payback in an average of 10.29 months.3 That is capex payback, which is a completely different and much harder test.

An independent analysis puts Lenskart's new-store payback nearer 17 months, with customer acquisition cost rising from ₹650 in FY23 to ₹850 in FY25 against an average order value of ₹1,900.10 That is one analyst's model rather than a filing, and it contradicts the RHP, so treat it as the sceptic's case rather than fact. But the gap between 10 and 17 months is roughly the difference between a good business and a treadmill.

When somebody quotes you a payback period, ask what is in the numerator. Fit-out only, or fit-out plus security deposit plus pre-opening cost plus the first three months of losses?

Rent that outruns revenue

The cleanest example in recent Indian accounts is GIVA. FY25 revenue of ₹518 crore, up 89%. Operating lease cost of ₹47 crore against ₹20 crore, up 134%. Net loss widened 23% to ₹72 crore.11

Rent is now about 9% of revenue and growing half again as fast as the top line. They are targeting a thousand stores by 2028, from 240 today. I am not predicting anything; I am pointing at a ratio. If rent grows faster than revenue for long enough, there is no sales number that fixes it, because the fixed cost base was set by the lease and the revenue was not.

The cash cycle is the cost nobody prices

This is the part I understood viscerally only after running a business with inventory rather than one with a Shopify checkout.

A digital-first brand converts cash in roughly 7 to 15 days. An offline business runs 90 to 150.12 General trade credit terms are 15 to 45 days, modern trade 30 to 60.13 You are now financing a working capital draw that did not exist before, on top of the capex and the lease, and you are doing it at whatever your cost of capital actually is rather than the number in your model.

At Priyabrat, the industrial side of my family's business, this is simply how life works. You bid, you supply, you wait, you chase receivables. I spent three years learning how to compress that cycle, and we got the win rate from 15% to 25% while halving receivables. What I did not appreciate until later is that a D2C founder going offline is signing up for exactly that world, usually without having run it before, and usually while telling investors the story is about brand.

Where it worked, and what was different

It plainly does work for some people, and the pattern is reasonably consistent.

Lenskart. Eighty percent of revenue is prescription glasses. You cannot buy prescription eyewear well without a fitting. The category requires the store. They also hold gross margin through vertical integration, with frame and lens costs 35 to 40% below the industry average.3 Store-level EBITDA of 33% is not an accident of execution; it is a structural property of selling something that needs a physical interaction anyway.

Snitch. FY25 revenue roughly doubled to around ₹500 crore, EBITDA up fivefold to ₹30 crore, retail moving from 30% to 40 to 45% of revenue. The number that matters: marketing spend fell 50% in the same year.14 In-store conversion runs 50 to 60% against low single digits online, and in-store AOV is about double. Offline replaced paid acquisition rather than sitting on top of it. (Reported FY25 revenue varies across sources between ₹498 and ₹520 crore, so take the precise figure loosely.)

Nykaa Beauty. Large-format destination stores in the top hundred cities, two-thirds of physical GMV from premium brands, 8.9% EBITDA margin. Meanwhile Nykaa Fashion runs at minus 8.3%.15 Same company, same capital, same playbook, opposite outcome, and the variable is the category.

The discriminator across all three is whether physical presence materially lifts conversion for what you sell. Eyewear, jewellery, apparel fit, beauty shade matching: yes. A seasoning jar: considerably less so, which is a conclusion I reached about my own brand and did not enjoy.

Where it did not

Sugar Cosmetics is the hardest case to look at. Two hundred brand-owned stores by mid-2023, the largest owned estate of any beauty brand in India at the time. Then revenue fell 20% to ₹404 crore in FY25 while EBITDA went from minus ₹48 crore to minus ₹116 crore, and the September 2026 round came in at a 75 to 80% valuation cut.16 The registered valuer's language, quoted by Inc42, was "a sustained and worsening pattern of financial deterioration." A 20% revenue decline produced an 80% valuation decline, and the gearing that turns one into the other is fixed offline cost.

A claim I am not going to repeat Several aggregators state that Sugar closed 30 to 40% of its newly opened stores. I could not find a primary source for that anywhere, and reporting from October 2025 still describes 200 stores with no closures. It may well be true. It is not established, so I am leaving it out.

Pepperfry built 200 offline studios, two-thirds company-owned, in premium neighbourhoods. The structural detail that undid them: all franchise-related costs, staff training, branding, marketing, sat on the company's balance sheet, so the franchise model delivered none of the capital-light benefit it was supposed to. FY24 revenue of ₹189 crore, down 30%, against a loss of ₹117 crore, of which ₹51 crore was depreciation largely on lease right-of-use assets. They spent ₹1.73 for every rupee of revenue and were sold in distress in September 2025, against a peak valuation of ₹3,100 crore.17

The Ayurveda Co gives the clearest statement anyone has made on the record. Twenty company-owned stores, 800 beauty advisor counters, 110 distributors, 10,000 retail touchpoints, 100-plus SKUs. The company shut down in 2025 and liquidated in 2026. Co-founder Param Bhargava said it "expanded too quickly across too many channels and hired senior executives before its operational systems were mature enough."18

That is the sentence. Not that offline was wrong, but that the organisation was not ready to run it.

Honasa is the case worth studying properly

Mamaearth's parent moved from a two-layer distribution structure to direct distributors in the top 50 cities under Project Neev, starting November 2023. In Q2 FY25 the clean-up produced a ₹63.5 crore sales returns provision, EBITDA of minus ₹31 crore against plus ₹40 crore a year earlier, and a net loss of ₹18.6 crore. The stock fell around 40% in a week.19,20

The quote that explains it:

"Most of our understanding was from the system inventory which we were seeing just for the superstockists." Varun Alagh, Honasa, on why the impact exceeded their estimate. They had guided to roughly 150 basis points and delivered around 1,100.21

They scaled a channel they could not see into. Primary sales looked like revenue. Nobody knew what was actually selling through to consumers, so nobody knew how much of it would come back.

The honest ending matters too, and most write-ups stop before it. By Q3 FY26, Honasa was at ₹601 crore of revenue, EBITDA up 151% to ₹65.5 crore, margin at 10.9%, direct reach at one lakh outlets and total reach at 2.7 lakh. FY26 closed at ₹2,475 crore of revenue and ₹200 crore of profit, with a first dividend.22,23 The channel became the growth engine. It just cost them two years and a reputation first.

The alternative almost nobody argues for

Here is the arithmetic that I find hardest to get past.

boAt reaches 12,000 retail stores through 112 distributors and owns zero stores.24 Lenskart spends about ₹44 lakh of capex plus a multi-year lease commitment for one.

India has roughly 12 million retail outlets.25 General trade is still about 80% of FMCG and modern trade 12 to 15%. The trade margin stack in general trade runs about 28% of MRP, roughly 10% to the distributor and 18% to the retailer, leaving the manufacturer about 67% of MRP net of GST.26 That is not cheap. It is also not 28% plus rent plus staff plus a 120-day cash cycle plus a 41% lock-in.

Sugar had 45,000 retail touchpoints at its peak. It also had 200 owned stores. The touchpoints were not what broke it.

The gates I would actually use

If someone asked me to write down when a brand has earned the right to open its first store, this is the list I would hand over. Every item is drawn from something above rather than from a framework.

  1. Can you state store-level contribution and the company margin you expect it to produce? Lenskart's 33% against 13.4% is the only public Indian benchmark. If you cannot name both, you are modelling half the problem.
  2. Is your payback measured on full capex, deposit and pre-opening cost? Not operational breakeven. They are different by a year or more.
  3. Does offline substitute for paid acquisition or add to it? Snitch's marketing spend halved in the year offline went from 30% to 45% of revenue. Sugar's did not. That is the cleanest discriminator in the data.
  4. Have you modelled the working capital draw? Seven to fifteen days becoming ninety to a hundred and fifty, at your real cost of capital.
  5. Is rent growing slower than revenue? Track the ratio from month one. GIVA's 134% against 89% is what the warning sign looks like.
  6. Does your category reward physical trial? Returns run 25 to 35% online against 5 to 8% in store for apparel.12 In a high-return category the store improves contribution margin. In a low-return category it only adds cost.
  7. Are you past roughly ₹50 to ₹75 crore in revenue? That is where trade press puts the inflection, and it is consistent with everything above.12

There is one more thing, and it is not a metric. Devangshu Dutta of Third Eyesight put it this way: it takes seven to eight years to build a brand, and most funds need an exit in three to five.27

I suspect that mismatch explains more premature store openings than any analytical failure does. The founder is not confused about the economics. The founder is responding to a clock that belongs to somebody else.

Which is a harder problem than a spreadsheet, and worth naming out loud before you sign a lease with a 41% lock-in.
